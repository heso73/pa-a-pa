Why small businesses fail: 7 mistakes to avoid before you start

A business rarely stops because of one big mistake. It is usually small errors in the numbers or in the preparation, made early, that add up. The good news is that you can spot them in advance with a pencil and a few figures.

In short
  • The most common mistakes are about price, break-even, cash and customers.
  • Almost all of them can be checked before you spend your first dollar.
  • A ten-minute test at the end of this page shows where to start.

1. Selling below the real cost

People forget their own time, packaging, transport, payment fees and contributions. Every sale then loses money, even when customers are happy.

What to do: work out the full cost of one product or job before you set the price.

2. Not knowing your break-even point

Without this figure you cannot tell whether your sales target covers your costs, and then your own pay. Many owners discover after six months that they needed to sell twice as much.

What to do: calculate how many sales you need each month. See fixed costs vs variable costs.

3. Running out of cash

A business can make a profit on paper and still be unable to pay its bills: customers who pay in 30 days, stock bought in advance, rent due before the first sale.

What to do: plan cash month by month and keep a reserve for slow months and late payers.

4. Starting without customers

Building first and looking for customers later. An idea that "everyone likes" is not a sale yet.

What to do: name five real customers, then ask each for a small commitment: a pre-order, a deposit or a firm date.

5. Forgetting taxes and contributions

The money in your account is not all yours. Part goes to taxes and social contributions, sometimes months after the sale.

What to do: set that share aside as soon as you are paid, in a separate account, and learn the due dates for your type of business.

6. Mixing personal and business money

Personal spending through the business account (and the reverse) hides the real numbers.

What to do: open a separate account for the business and pay yourself a fixed amount each month, like a salary.

7. Growing too fast

Hiring, renting premises or investing before sales are steady raises your fixed costs and your break-even point. One slow month can then put the business in trouble.

What to do: wait for several months of sales above break-even, add one thing at a time, and estimate the extra sales it must bring before you spend.

A ten-minute test

Answer these five questions in writing. If you hesitate on one, start there:

  • How much does one sale really cost, all in?
  • How much must I sell each month to cover my costs and pay myself?
  • How many months can I last without income?
  • Who are my first five customers, by name?
  • What share of my takings is not mine (taxes, contributions, sales tax)?

Frequently asked questions

What is the number one reason small businesses fail?

Analyses of business failures keep coming back to three causes: not enough customers, not enough cash and badly calculated prices. The order varies by sector; this guide covers all three.

Can I check my business idea before I start?

Yes. Work out your cost price, your break-even point and your cash needs, and test the idea with real customers. Pa a Pa Caribbean offers a free viability test with no sign-up.

Do I need a business plan to avoid failure?

A long document is not essential to begin, but a simple forecast of sales, costs and cash is.
